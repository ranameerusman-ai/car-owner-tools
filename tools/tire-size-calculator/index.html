Introduction

New tires are one of the most rewarding upgrades for your car, and the right size keeps your speed, handling and wheel clearance working exactly as designed.

This calculator compares your current tire size with a new one, side by side. Enter both sizes and you will see the change in diameter and circumference, your real speed when the speedometer reads 60 mph, and whether the new size is within the generally accepted 3% limit — before you buy.

How the size is worked out

Every tire size is calculated from the three numbers printed on the sidewall.

Sidewall height = width × aspect ratio ÷ 100. Overall diameter = rim diameter × 25.4 mm, plus twice the sidewall height. Circumference = π × diameter. Your real speed is your shown speed × new circumference ÷ old circumference.

For example, a 205/55 R16 tire has a sidewall of 205 × 55 ÷ 100 = 112.75 mm, and a diameter of 16 × 25.4 + 2 × 112.75 = 631.9 mm.

Tips before you buy

Three quick checks that save trouble later.

CHECK FIRST

The door sticker

Your car’s maker lists approved tire sizes on the sticker inside the driver’s door frame. Start there — those sizes are guaranteed to fit.

MATCH THE RATINGS

Load index and speed rating

A new tire should match or beat the load index and speed rating of your original tires. Choosing a tire that matches the rating gives you the best value and the safest drive.

TELL YOUR INSURER

A size change is worth sharing

Changing tire size can change your insurance cover, so sharing the change with your insurer keeps everything in order before you buy.

How to read a tire size

A tire marked 205/55 R16 means: 205 mm wide, sidewall height is 55% of the width, and it fits a 16-inch rim. The “R” stands for radial construction, which almost all modern car tires use.

STEP 1

Find your current size

Look on the sidewall of your tire, or on the sticker inside the driver’s door frame, or in your owner’s manual.

STEP 2

Enter the new size

Type in the size you are thinking of buying — a wider tire, a lower profile, or a bigger rim.

STEP 3

Check the difference

If the diameter change is within 3%, the new size is generally considered safe to use. Bigger changes affect your speedometer, gearing and clearance.

What the numbers mean

Three numbers decide whether a new tire size is a good fit for your car.

KEEP WITHIN ±3%

Diameter change

The most important number. A bigger diameter raises your car and shifts the speedometer reading; a smaller one shifts it the other way. Stay within 3% and your car keeps its smooth, accurate feel.

SPEEDOMETER

Real speed vs shown speed

Your speedometer is calibrated for your original tire size. With bigger tires you are actually going faster than it shows — which matters for speed limits and speeding tickets.

CHECK CLEARANCE

Width and rim size

A wider tire or bigger rim can rub against the wheel arch or suspension. Always check the clearance and that the rim fits your car’s bolt pattern before buying.

Frequently asked questions

Can I put a different tire size on my car?

Yes, within limits. A diameter change of up to about 3% is generally considered acceptable. Bigger changes can affect your speedometer accuracy, fuel economy, handling and whether the tire clears the wheel arch — and may affect your cover, so check first.

Will bigger tires change my speedometer reading?

Yes. Bigger tires cover more ground per turn, so your car travels further than the speedometer shows. This calculator shows your real speed at an indicated 60 mph so you can see exactly how it changes.

Where do I find my current tire size?

On the sidewall of the tire itself (for example 205/55 R16), on the sticker on the driver’s door frame, inside the fuel filler cap, or in your owner’s manual.

Does tire size affect fuel economy?

It can. Wider tires usually have more rolling resistance, which can lower MPG slightly. A much bigger diameter also changes your effective gearing. The effect is usually small for a change within 3%.

Will a bigger tire affect my odometer?

Yes. Your odometer counts how many times the wheels turn. Bigger tires cover more ground per turn, so the odometer records fewer miles than you actually drive — and smaller tires do the opposite.
