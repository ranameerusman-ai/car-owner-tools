Introduction

A new car starts its journey at full value, and the value changes fastest in the first few years. Knowing what your car is worth now helps you decide when to sell, what insurance cover you need, and whether that new car is worth the price.

This calculator works out your car’s current value from what you paid, how old it is, and an average yearly depreciation rate. The result is an estimate — the real market value also depends on mileage, condition and demand — but it shows you the pattern clearly: the first years bring the biggest change.

How the value is calculated

The value changes by the same percentage every year, applied to the current value each time.

Value after one year = price × (1 − rate). Repeat for each year. At a 15% rate, a $30,000 car is worth $25,500 after one year, $21,675 after two, and about $18,424 after three — the same figures shown in the year-by-year table above.

How to keep more value

Your car's value journey is yours to shape — a few smart habits keep more of it with you.

KEEP THE RECORDS

Service history matters

A full service history and receipts show a buyer the car was looked after, and they protect its value.

MILEAGE COUNTS

Drive less, keep more

High mileage changes value faster than the average rate. Keeping annual miles low keeps more value in your car.

FIX SMALL MARKS

Before you sell

Small dents, scratches and worn tires are quick wins — fixing them before you sell adds more to the price than the repair costs.

How does car value change over time?

The pattern is remarkably consistent across brands — the first years bring the biggest change.

~20% IN YEAR 1

The first year

A new car typically keeps about four-fifths of its value after the first year, with the biggest change in the first months.

~40–50% BY YEAR 3

Three years in

After three years most cars are worth roughly half what they cost new. This is why 3-year-old cars are often the best value buys.

SLOWS AFTER YEAR 5

Later years

Value changes ease a lot after five years. A well-kept 8-year-old car keeps nearly all of its value each year.

BRAND MATTERS

Some hold value better

Toyota, Honda and some trucks and SUVs hold value better than average; luxury cars and large sedans usually change value fastest.

MILEAGE COUNTS

Miles and condition

High mileage, heavy wear and poor condition all change value faster than the average rate used here.

DEMAND SHIFTS

Market swings

Used-car prices move with the wider market. In a shortage, even older cars can hold value unusually well.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate car depreciation?

Multiply the purchase price by (1 − annual rate) for each year. At a 15% rate, a $30,000 car is worth $25,500 after one year, $21,675 after two, and about $18,424 after three.

What is a normal depreciation rate?

Most cars keep 80–85% of their value a year in the first few years, then 88–92% a year after that. This calculator uses one average rate — set it higher for luxury cars, lower for models known to hold value.

When does a car's value settle?

The yearly change becomes small after 8–10 years. Very old cars in good condition can even rise in value if they become collectable.

Is it better to buy new or used?

On value alone, used wins: the first-year change is already behind you. Buying a 2–3 year old car often gets you most of the car for roughly half the price.

How do I find my car’s real market value?

Check what similar cars — same model, year and mileage — are advertised for in your area. This calculator gives an estimate from the rate you set; the market price is what buyers are actually paying.
