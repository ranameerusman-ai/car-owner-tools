How much value do cars lose?

The pattern is remarkably consistent across brands — the first years hurt the most.

~20% IN YEAR 1

The first year

A new car typically loses around a fifth of its value in the first year, mostly the moment it leaves the dealership.

~40–50% BY YEAR 3

Three years in

After three years most cars are worth roughly half what they cost new. This is why 3-year-old cars are often the best value buys.

SLOWS AFTER YEAR 5

Later years

Depreciation slows a lot after five years. A well-kept 8-year-old car loses only a few percent of its value per year.

BRAND MATTERS

Some hold value better

Toyota, Honda and some trucks and SUVs hold value better than average; luxury cars and large sedans usually lose value fastest.

MILEAGE COUNTS

Miles and condition

High mileage, accidents and poor condition all push value down faster than the average rate used here.

DEMAND SHIFTS

Market swings

Used-car prices rise and fall with the wider market. In a shortage, even older cars can hold value unusually well.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate car depreciation?

Multiply the purchase price by (1 − annual rate) for each year. At a 15% rate, a $30,000 car is worth $25,500 after one year, $21,675 after two, and about $18,424 after three.

What is a normal depreciation rate?

Most cars lose 15–20% a year in the first few years, then 8–12% a year after that. This calculator uses one average rate — set it higher for luxury cars, lower for models known to hold value.

When does a car stop losing value?

It never fully stops, but the yearly loss becomes small after 8–10 years. Very old cars in good condition can even rise in value if they become collectable.

Is it better to buy new or used?

On depreciation alone, used wins: someone else paid for the steep first-year drop. Buying a 2–3 year old car often gets you most of the car for roughly half the price.
