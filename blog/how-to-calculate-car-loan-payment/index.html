How to Calculate a Car Loan Payment

Car LoansNovember 22, 2026 · 9 min read

Your monthly car loan payment is the number that decides whether a car fits your budget — not the sticker price. Two cars with the same price can have very different payments, because the interest rate, the loan term and your down payment all change the maths.

The good news is that the calculation is simple once you know the formula. This guide shows you how to calculate a car loan payment step by step, with real examples you can check, what makes your payment higher or lower, and how to get a payment you can afford.

What Is a Car Loan Payment?

A car loan payment is the fixed amount you pay every month until the loan is paid off. Each payment is split into two parts: part pays the interest the lender charges, and part pays down the amount you borrowed (the principal). Early in the loan, more of your payment goes to interest; later on, more goes to the principal.

Most car loans are fixed-rate installment loans. That means the interest rate stays the same for the whole loan, and the monthly payment never changes. You know exactly what you will pay every month, from the first payment to the last.

Three things decide your payment: how much you borrow, the interest rate (APR), and how many months you take to repay it. Change any one of them, and the payment changes too.

The Car Loan Payment Formula

The formula looks long, but it only needs three numbers. Here it is:

Monthly payment = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

Here is what each letter means:

  • P is the principal — the amount you borrow. That is the car price minus your down payment and any trade-in value, plus any taxes and fees you roll into the loan.
  • r is the monthly interest rate. Take the APR (annual percentage rate) and divide it by 12. A 6% APR means r = 0.06 ÷ 12 = 0.005.
  • n is the number of monthly payments. A 5-year loan means n = 60, a 4-year loan means n = 48, and a 6-year loan means n = 72.

You can work this out on any scientific calculator or in a spreadsheet. In a spreadsheet, the PMT function does the same job: =PMT(6%/12, 60, -25000) gives the payment for a $25,000 loan at 6% APR over 60 months.

How to Calculate a Car Loan Payment in 3 Steps

Let us work through one example together: a $25,000 loan at 6% APR, paid back over 60 months (5 years).

Step 1 — Find your three numbers

First, write down the amount you are borrowing (P), the monthly interest rate (r) and the number of payments (n). For our example: P = $25,000, APR = 6% so r = 0.06 ÷ 12 = 0.005, and n = 60.

Step 2 — Work out (1 + r)n

Add 1 to the monthly rate and raise it to the power of the number of payments: (1 + 0.005)60 = 1.34885. This is the part that accounts for interest building up over time. A calculator with a power button (xy) does this in one step.

Step 3 — Put the numbers into the formula

Now plug everything in: $25,000 × 0.005 × 1.34885 ÷ (1.34885 − 1) = $483.32 a month. That is your monthly payment. Over 60 months you will pay $28,999.20 in total, which means $3,999.20 of it is interest.

That is the whole method. Once you can do it for one loan, you can compare any two loans side by side in minutes.

A small difference in rate is worth real money, which is why it pays to compare offers from more than one lender.

Shorter Term or Longer Term?

Stretching a loan over more months always lowers the monthly payment — but it always raises the total interest you pay. Here is the same $25,000 loan at 6% APR on four different terms:

TermMonthly paymentTotal paidTotal interest
36 months$760.55$27,379.74$2,379.74
48 months$587.13$28,182.03$3,182.03
60 months$483.32$28,999.20$3,999.20
72 months$414.32$29,831.20$4,831.20

The 72-month loan looks friendly at $414.32 a month, but it costs $2,451.46 more in interest than the 36-month loan. The 36-month loan costs the least overall, but $760.55 a month is a big commitment. Most buyers pick the middle ground: the shortest term whose payment still fits comfortably in their monthly budget.

There is another catch with long loans. Cars lose value fastest in the first few years, so a 72- or 84-month loan can leave you owing more than the car is worth for a long time. What Makes Your Car Loan Payment Higher or Lower?

Five things move your payment. Knowing them helps you change the ones you can control.

Loan amount

The more you borrow, the more you pay each month. Every extra $1,000 borrowed adds roughly $19 a month on a 60-month loan at 6% APR. Choosing a less expensive car — or the same car with fewer options — is the fastest way to cut the payment.

Interest rate (APR)

The APR is the price of borrowing, and it depends mostly on your credit score and the lender. A better credit score usually means a lower APR. Even a small drop in rate saves money every single month, so check your credit report before you shop and compare at least three offers, including your bank or credit union.

Loan term

More months means a smaller payment but more interest overall, as the table above shows. Shorter terms cost more each month but much less in total. Pick the shortest term you can afford without stretching your budget.

Down payment

Money you pay up front is money you do not borrow, so it lowers the payment directly. A bigger down payment also protects you from owing more than the car is worth. Many lenders and dealers suggest at least 20% down on a new car and 10% on a used car.

Trade-in value

Trading in your old car works like a down payment: its value comes off the amount you need to borrow. A $4,000 trade-in on a $25,000 car means you only finance $21,000, which cuts the payment by about $77 a month on a 60-month loan at 6% APR.

How a Down Payment Changes Your Payment

Here is what a down payment does in real numbers. Take a $30,000 car at 6% APR over 60 months:

  • No down payment: you borrow $30,000 and pay $579.98 a month.
  • $6,000 down (20%): you borrow $24,000 and pay $463.99 a month.

That is about $116 less every month, and about $960 less paid in total over the life of the loan. The down payment does not just lower the payment — it lowers the interest too, because you are borrowing less money in the first place.

What Is a Good Car Loan Payment?

There is no single number that is right for everyone, but a well-known rule of thumb can help: the 20/4/10 rule. Put at least 20% down, keep the loan to 4 years (48 months) or less, and keep your total car costs — payment, insurance and fuel — under 10% of your gross monthly income. So if you earn $5,000 a month before tax, your total car costs should stay under $500.

That is a guide, not a law. The real test is your own budget: after rent, bills, food and savings, is there room for the payment every month, even in a bad month? If a payment only fits when everything goes perfectly, it is too high. A payment you can make comfortably is worth more than a nicer car you have to worry about.

And do not judge a loan by the monthly payment alone. Dealers sometimes stretch the term to make the payment look small, while the total cost quietly grows. Always ask for the total amount you will pay, not just the monthly figure.

How to Lower Your Monthly Payment

If the payment on the car you want is too high, you have several good options:

  • Save a bigger down payment before you buy — even $1,000 extra helps.
  • Improve your credit score first, then re-shop for a lower APR.
  • Compare offers from banks, credit unions and online lenders, not just the dealer.
  • Choose a less expensive car or a lower trim of the same model.
  • Trade in your current car instead of selling it privately, if the numbers work.
  • Negotiate the car price before you talk about financing — a lower price means a smaller loan.

One thing to avoid: do not lower the payment by stretching the loan to 84 months unless you have no other choice. The payment drops, but the interest climbs and you stay in debt on a car that keeps losing value.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate a car loan payment by hand?

Use the loan payment formula: monthly payment = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1), where P is the amount you borrow, r is the monthly interest rate (APR divided by 12), and n is the number of monthly payments. For example, a $25,000 loan at 6% APR over 60 months is $483.32 a month.

What is a good monthly car payment?

A common rule of thumb is the 20/4/10 rule: put at least 20% down, keep the loan to 4 years or less, and keep the payment (plus insurance and fuel) under 10% of your gross monthly income. The right number for you depends on your budget, so check what you can afford after all your other bills.

Does a longer car loan lower my monthly payment?

Yes. Spreading the same loan over more months lowers the monthly payment, but you pay more interest in total. For a $25,000 loan at 6% APR, a 36-month term costs $760.55 a month and $2,379.74 in interest, while a 72-month term costs $414.32 a month but $4,831.20 in interest.

How much does a down payment lower my car payment?

A bigger down payment means a smaller loan, so the payment drops. On a $30,000 car at 6% APR over 60 months, borrowing the full $30,000 costs $579.98 a month, while a $6,000 down payment leaves a $24,000 loan at $463.99 a month — about $116 less every month.

Is the interest rate or the loan term more important?

Both matter, but the interest rate usually matters more over the life of the loan. On a $25,000 loan over 60 months, 4% APR costs $460.41 a month while 9% APR costs $518.96 — a difference of about $58 a month and more than $3,500 in total interest. Compare both when you shop for a loan.
