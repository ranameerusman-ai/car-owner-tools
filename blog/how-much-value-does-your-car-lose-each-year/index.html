How Much Value Does Your Car Lose Each Year?

Car Value

20 November 2026 · 5 min read

Depreciation — the value your car loses as it ages — is usually the biggest single cost of owning a car, bigger than fuel or insurance. Yet most buyers never work it out before they buy. Here is the pattern, and how to lose less of it.

The pattern

A new car typically loses around 20% of its value in the first year, and roughly 40–50% by the end of year three. After that the yearly loss slows to 8–12%, and after 8–10 years it becomes small. A $30,000 car at a 15% yearly rate is worth about $25,500 after one year, $21,675 after two, and $18,424 after three.

Why the first year hurts

Most of the first-year drop happens the moment the car leaves the dealership: a buyer will pay less for a “used” car than a new one, even if it has only a few miles on it. Taxes, dealer fees and the new-car premium all vanish from the resale value at once.

Which cars hold value best?

Toyota, Honda and some trucks and SUVs hold value better than average, helped by reliability and strong demand for used models. Luxury cars and large sedans usually lose value fastest. Electric cars have been more volatile, because battery and technology changes move demand quickly.

How to lose less

  • Buy 2–3 years old: someone else paid for the steep first drop; you get most of the car for roughly half the price.
  • Keep mileage low and condition good: high mileage and poor condition push value down faster than the average rate.
  • Pick a model that holds value: check used prices for 3-year-old versions before you buy new.
  • Maintain it: a full service history is worth real money at resale time.

The depreciation curve, year by year

Depreciation is not a straight line. It is steep at first and flattens out — which is why the numbers below matter more than any single percentage. For a $30,000 car losing 15% a year: after one year it is worth about $25,500, after two years $21,675, after three years $18,424, after five years $13,311, and after eight years about $8,175. Notice the pattern: the car loses $4,500 in year one but only about $1,450 in year eight. That is why keeping a car for longer is usually the cheapest option — the expensive years are already behind you.

New vs nearly new: the maths

Buying a two-year-old car instead of a new one means someone else paid for the steepest drop. A $30,000 new car is worth about $21,675 after two years — so buying that same car at two years old saves roughly $8,300, and you still get a car with most of its life left. The trade-off is a shorter warranty and slightly older technology, but for most buyers the saving is worth far more. The same maths works in reverse: if you sell a car after only one year, you take the biggest loss of all.

Do electric cars depreciate differently?

Electric cars have been less predictable. Some held value well when demand was high and supply was short; others dropped fast when makers cut new prices or new models arrived with much better range. Battery health matters too: a car with a degraded battery is worth far less. The general rule still applies — buy a model with strong used demand and a battery in good health — but check actual used prices rather than guessing, because the electric market moves faster than the petrol one.
