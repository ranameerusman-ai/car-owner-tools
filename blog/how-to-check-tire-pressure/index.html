How to Check Your Tire Pressure (and Why It Matters)

Tires

20 November 2026 · 5 min read

Five minutes, once a month, can save fuel, make your tires last longer and keep you safer. Most drivers check their tires far too rarely — and under-inflated tires are one of the most common reasons cars use more fuel than they should.

What pressure should your tires be?

The correct pressure is not the number printed on the tire sidewall — that is the maximum the tire can take. The right figure for your car is on a sticker inside the driver’s door, or in the fuel filler cap, or in your owner’s manual. It is usually around 30–35 PSI for a normal car, and often different for the front and rear tires.

How to check it

  1. Check when the tires are cold. Driving warms tires up and raises the pressure reading. Morning, before you drive, is best.
  2. Unscrew the valve cap on one tire and keep it somewhere safe.
  3. Press the gauge firmly onto the valve. A cheap digital gauge costs a few dollars and is more accurate than the one at a petrol station.
  4. Read the number and compare it with the door sticker. If it is low, add air; if it is high, press the small pin in the valve to let a little out.
  5. Repeat for all four tires, including the spare if you have one, and screw the caps back on.

Why it matters

Under-inflated tires bend more as they roll, which wastes fuel — typically 1–3% for every 10% the pressure is low — and wears the edges out early. They also grip worse in the wet. Over-inflated tires wear out in the middle and give a harsher ride. Either way, the fix costs nothing but five minutes.

How often?

Once a month, and before any long trip. Tires lose a little air naturally, and faster in cold weather — make it a habit on the first of the month.

What about the TPMS warning light?

Most cars built since 2007 have a tire pressure monitoring system (TPMS) that lights up when a tire is low. It is a useful warning, but no replacement for checking: the light usually only comes on once pressure has dropped by about 25%. By then you have already been wasting fuel and wearing your tires, and on many cars the system cannot even tell you which tire is low — you still need a gauge.

Tire pressure in winter and summer

Air shrinks when it gets cold: for roughly every 10°F (about 5°C) the temperature drops, tire pressure falls by about 1 PSI. That is why the warning light often appears on the first cold morning of autumn — nothing is wrong, the tire just needs topping up. Check more often in winter, and always use the door-sticker figure. In summer, do not let air out of a warm tire that reads high — check it cold in the morning instead.

What uneven wear tells you

While you are down there, look at the tread. Wear on both edges usually means under-inflation; wear down the middle usually means over-inflation; wear on one edge only can mean the wheels need aligning. Catching these signs early saves money: an unevenly worn tire can need replacing thousands of miles before its time.
